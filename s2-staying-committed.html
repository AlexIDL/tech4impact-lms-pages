The first “no”

Ana, Jonas and Mira visit ten local shops to ask if they would participate in their social venture. One says “yes”, while another says “maybe later.” Eight others say no.

Jonas wants to stop. “Maybe this wasn’t a good idea after all,” she says.

This is a very common reaction for entrepreneurs when facing resistance to their idea. Many social enterprises reach this point and their founders have to decide whether to continue. Three competences can help here: passion, resilience and adaptability.

Passion: why you care

Passion means caring deeply about your cause. For Ana, Jonas and Mira, it is the thought of families going without food while good food is thrown away. Passion gives you energy and a reason to continue when the work, and times, are hard.

But not all passion is the same. Researchers describe two kinds (Vallerand et al., 2003).

Healthy passion Passion that consumes you
You choose to do the work. You feel you must do it, whatever the cost.
It fits in with the rest of your life. It takes away from your sleep, friends and other duties.
You can listen when others disagree. You find it hard to hear criticism.
Setbacks are disappointing, but you recover. Setbacks feel like a personal disaster.

You can probably see how one might be beneficial when starting or working in a social venture while the other might be detrimental. Take a moment and think about your own passions. Do you find yourself leaning one way or the other?

Resilience: recovering from adversity

Resilience is the ability to recover and keep going after a setback, problem or failure. Some people mistakenly think that resilient people are simply “tough”, but being tough is just a small aspect of being resilient. In fact, being resilient is never about ignoring problems or hiding feelings. Rather, it’s about facing those problems, working through your emotions and still deciding to move forward.

This is backed by research showing that resilience is a learned competence, not an innate ability (Masten, 2001).

Five habits that help develop resilience

  • 1
    Name the problem. “Eight shops said no” is the reality of the situation, but that is not the same as “We failed”.
  • 2
    Look for information in the setback. Why did the shops say “no”? You might be able to find a consistent reason and fix it.
  • 3
    Break the next step into something small. Setbacks can lead to knee-jerk reactions as emotion takes over from logic. Slow things down and make your next step an incremental one, not a life-changing one.
  • 4
    Ask for support. Talk to trusted advisors, mentors and teammates. Getting the opinion from someone removed from the emotional aspect of a failure can help you avoid rash decisions.
  • 5
    Remember your reason. Ground yourself by remembering why you got involved in a social venture to begin with.

Back to the team

All three members of the team are disappointed with the results but decide to turn the “no”s into a positive. Ana goes back to the shops and asks why they said no. Most say the same thing: they are worried about the legal consequences if someone gets ill from the donated food.

It turns out the problem was not their idea, but a clear, fixable one. The team takes time to research local food ordinances and finds a rule that protects food donors from liability. They print out the ordinance and return to the stores; four more shops agree to participate.

Resilience did not mean the team could not feel disappointed — they clearly were. Yet instead of letting that disappointment overwhelm them, they took steps that allowed them to work through it and turn the situation around. Competences go up and down. You may feel particularly resilient one day and less so on another. What is key is having a set of steps to take, trusted people to confide in, and the ability to ground yourself in why you joined a social venture to begin with.
