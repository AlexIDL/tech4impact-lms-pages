A visit to the kitchen

With several shops now participating, the SharedTable team decides it is time to focus on the community kitchen their app is meant to serve. Arriving at the kitchen, they expect a warm welcome. Instead, the kitchen manager, Yolanda, is polite but guarded.

“We’ve had people come in here wanting to help before,” she says. “They mean well, but they always come with solutions without ever asking what we actually need.”

Ana had assumed the kitchen’s biggest problem was getting enough food. Yolanda explains that finding food is only part of it. The harder problem is unpredictability. The kitchen plans meals in advance — when donations arrive larger or smaller than promised, feeding people reliably becomes very difficult.

As the team leaves, Jonas says: “It looks like we only understood half the problem.”

What is empathy?

Empathy is the ability to understand how another person feels and why. It means stepping out of your own perspective and into someone else’s — not necessarily to agree with them, but to genuinely understand their experience.

Empathy is often confused with sympathy. They are not the same thing.

Sympathy

Feeling concern for someone from your own point of view. You acknowledge their situation but keep a distance from it. “I’m sorry you’re going through that” — but you haven’t tried to understand it from their perspective.

Empathy

Trying to understand another person’s experience from their perspective. You listen before you conclude. You ask questions. You can say “I understand why you feel that way” — and mean it.

Both have their place, but in social entrepreneurship, empathy is the more powerful tool. It is the difference between building something for someone and building something with them.

Why it matters in a social venture

Social enterprises exist to solve real problems for real people. If you don’t understand the problem from their perspective — not just your assumptions, but their lived experience — you risk solving the wrong problem entirely.

The SharedTable team assumed scarcity was the kitchen’s core problem. But there were two issues: scarcity and predictability. Both require completely different solutions. An app that connects donors with the kitchen might reduce scarcity — but it does nothing about unpredictability unless you design for it.

Designing without empathy means solving the problem you imagined someone has. Designing with empathy means solving the problem they actually have.

How to practise empathy

Like every competence in this module, empathy can be developed. Here are four techniques that help.

1

Listen to understand, not just to respond

Most of us listen while already forming our next point. Active listening means focusing entirely on what the other person is saying — including what they say between the words. Silence, hesitation and tone all carry information.

2

Ask “what” and “how” questions

“What does a difficult day look like for you?” opens up experience. “How does that affect the people you serve?” connects it to impact. These questions invite open responses rather than a simple yes or no.

3

Suspend your assumptions

Go into every conversation knowing that your assumptions are probably incomplete. Your goal is to replace them with what you actually hear.

4

Reflect back what you heard

Before you leave a conversation, summarise your understanding and check it with the person. “So if I understand correctly, the biggest challenge isn’t just the quantity of food — it’s being able to plan around it. Is that right?” This confirms understanding and shows respect.

Back to the team

The team returns to the kitchen twice more — this time to listen rather than present. They learn that Yolanda’s most practical need is advance notice. Even 24 hours’ warning of what food is coming allows her team to plan meals properly. They also discover a third challenge: storage. Some donations require refrigeration the kitchen doesn’t always have capacity for.

None of these things were on the team’s mind when they started SharedTable. They came directly from listening.

The team updates the app: donors now post a food pickup request at least 24 hours in advance, and a simple confirmation step allows the kitchen to confirm whether they can accept the donation.

Empathy does not require you to have lived the same experience as the people you are trying to help. It requires genuine curiosity about their experience, a willingness to suspend what you think you know, and honesty about the gap between your perspective and theirs. In a social venture, the people you serve are your best source of insight. Empathy is how you access it.
