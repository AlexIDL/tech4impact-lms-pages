Meet the team

Ana, Jonas and Mira are students. They want to help their town reduce food waste. Local shops throw away good food every day that could be used in a local community kitchen that is not able to provide enough food for the people in need.

Together, they decide to create a simple app called Shared Table. Shops post their unused food, and the kitchen collects it.

As technical students, building the app was the easy part. It’s everything else that is proving difficult: understanding the needs of the kitchen and the people they serve, working together when they disagree, and dealing with rejection when a shop decides not to work with them.

These are not technical skills. They are transversal competences, sometimes referred to as soft skills.

What are transversal competences?

Transversal competences are skills you can use in any job, project or in the day-to-day living of one’s life. They are not tied to one subject and are used by programmers, bakers, doctors and entrepreneurs.

In a social enterprise they become even more important. If your goal is to create social change, you’ll interact and work with people, face uncertainty and doubt, adapt to changing environments and constantly be balancing risk.

Twelve competences at a glance

Competence What it means
Self-awarenessKnowing your strengths, limits and values
ConfidenceBelieving you can do what you set out to do
PassionCaring deeply about your cause
ResilienceRecovering after a problem or failure
Critical thinkingQuestioning information before you accept it
AdaptabilityChanging your plan when conditions change
Risk managementSpotting what could go wrong and preparing for it
EmpathyUnderstanding how another person feels and why
CommunicationSharing ideas clearly, in speech and writing
CollaborationWorking well with others towards a shared goal
NegotiationFinding an agreement when people want different things
LeadershipHelping a group move toward a goal

This table is not a comprehensive list of every transversal competence. You also do not need to be strong in every competence. Nobody is. Most people are stronger in some and weaker in others.

Start with yourself

Ana

Confident when speaking in public, but gets stressed when she doesn’t have time to prepare.

Jonas

Listens well, but lacks confidence when speaking up or sharing an opinion that conflicts with others.

Mira

Has a lot of good ideas, but has trouble putting them into action.

Each of them has strengths and areas to develop. Knowing their strengths and what they need to work on is called self-awareness — and it’s the first step in building any transversal competence.

Competences can be learned. They are not fixed traits, but skills like any other that can be developed through practice, feedback and reflection. Unlike knowledge, transversal competences do not develop in a linear way — they rise and fall depending on the context, experience and people around you. They are also interconnected: you will often use multiple transversal competences in combination with each other.
